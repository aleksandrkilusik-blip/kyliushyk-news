My Path

From Scout to Missionary

My name is Oleksandr. I came to Christ in Odesa, as a student, through a campus outreach called "Who Am I?" — a Cru staff member shared the Gospel with me. That moment became my spiritual birth and changed the entire direction of my life.

For the next seven years, I served students in Odesa myself. I led the same "Who Am I?" outreach that once brought me to Christ — around twenty students went through it with me. I led discipleship, organized evangelistic events, and played baseball with the guys: sport turned out to be a door that preaching couldn't open. One of the first people I shared the Gospel with was Kostiantyn Shevchenko. Today he leads the student ministry in Odesa himself.

Later I spent some time in sales. The war in Donbas continued, but for many, life stayed ordinary. Russia's full-scale invasion in 2022 changed everything: I was mobilized and served as a scout in an assault brigade.

During one of my combat missions, I was severely wounded and lost my leg.

In rehab, there was no one beside me who would simply listen. I saw the same thing in dozens of my brothers-in-arms: people searching for meaning and community, finding emptiness instead. That's when I understood who I wanted to be for them — the person I was missing. Student ministry still matters to me, but a veteran will hear another veteran faster than anyone else.

That's why I joined the military ministry of Ukraine for Christ (Cru) — to share the Gospel with veterans, people wounded in body and soul, but open to the truth. Only Christ can heal the deepest wounds.

Why Veterans?

Reaching Ukraine's Veterans With the Hope of Christ

I know what it's like to come home wounded — in body and in spirit. That's what pulled me into this work: not a statistic, but faces I recognize, because I was one of them.

Many veterans return with pain, isolation, and an inner emptiness. The church is a place where they can find their Creator, feel accepted, and find a community that helps them reintegrate into society.

Today Ukraine has more than 1.3 million veterans — many carrying deep wounds, seen and unseen. After the war, that number could grow to 5–6 million. That's why I joined Ukraine for Christ — to be present, to listen to their stories, and to point them to the One who can restore.

Building This Ministry Together

Military Ministry is the veterans' outreach of Ukraine for Christ. Most of us have been through the war ourselves. Together we run retreats, visit hospitals, and support one another — I don't serve alone.

What I Do

How I Serve

Retreats and the podcast are the doors in. What matters most happens behind them: right now I'm walking closely with five veterans — not one-off meetings, but ongoing friendship, prayer, and time.

Presence in the Hospitals

My main goal in the hospitals is simply to be present. Sometimes there's something small to leave behind as a keepsake, but the real value of these visits is the time we spend together — time to truly listen, to share the pain, and to pray where it's needed most. I want every wounded soldier to know: what's happening inside him matters, and he's not alone in it.

The "Without Armor" Podcast

A space for honest conversation, without cynicism. We look for hope without pretending, and talk openly about people, war, and faith — without armor. I co-host the podcast with Nadiia Kalachova. It's produced in partnership with the Ukrainian Baptist Theological Seminary and the Ukraine for Christ mission.

Roman Pikulyk — Faith on the Front Line

In this episode we take off the armor with Roman Pikulyk — a veteran of the Russo-Ukrainian war since 2014 and a leader in Ukraine for Christ's military ministry. We talk honestly about what happens to faith on the front line, and whether a "clean," untouched Christianity is even possible there.

Video in Ukrainian, no subtitles.

A Thought for You
"Can you keep an untouched Christianity in a trench, where every glance is a matter of life and death?"

We don't avoid the hard edges: the experience of war that shapes and breaks at once, how faith affects leadership in the military, resentment toward the church, triggers in civilian life, and what a good path back into community can look like for a veteran.

Danik: A Path to Recovery

Danik is a former prisoner of war. He suffered serious injuries at 19. Today he's 23, living an active life, and by God's grace has a Christian community around him. I'm grateful for his friendship.

Two More Stories
Hospital · Rehabilitation

Yevhen: Two Hours in the Dark

Yevhen spent three years at war as a tank crewman. A shell exploded nearby — shrapnel in his head, long surgeries, and he lost his sight. During rehab he couldn't sit still in his room, so we took him to the movies — with an app that narrates the screen. And we put on blindfolds ourselves, and watched it together with him.

"Sometimes, to truly be beside someone, you have to step into their darkness for two hours."

"Without Armor" Podcast

Diana: A Pause Between Rotations

We recorded a podcast episode with Diana during a short pause between her rotations — those few weeks when soldiers try to fit an entire civilian life into almost no time. She told me what helps her keep from bringing the war home with her: family close by, and a clear structure to her days. Learning to separate war from peacetime life isn't indifference — it's serious inner work.

How Cru’s Support-Raising Model Works

Ukraine for Christ operates as a faith-based mission: our staff rely on God to provide for their ministry through a team of partners. That’s why every missionary — myself included — is responsible for building a team of people who give regularly toward their own work.

Cru has about 19,000 staff serving worldwide today, and most of them are supported this way — through individual partners rather than a general fund. Most Christians, it turns out, would rather give toward a specific ministry than an anonymous pool. Giving to a missionary directly lets you see the real fruit of your part in the Gospel going out.
